I spent about 14 years managing engineers. Leading teams, setting direction, running planning cycles, doing all the things that are supposed to make you a worse engineer over time.

That's the story, anyway.

<!--more-->

It's more complicated than that. I never fully stopped — side projects throughout, staying close to the work. But coding nights and weekends isn't the same as when it's the job. The pace is different. The depth of focus is different.

Coming back to building full time, I expected to feel that gap. Slower to implement, out of touch with the stack, second-guessing myself on technical calls. Some of that was true — I did second-guess my own judgment more than I should have. That's the honest cost.

A lot of what I was doing felt familiar, though. Not "I remember how to code" familiar. More like I'd been doing this all along — just with people instead of systems.

When you manage engineers, you learn that vague asks get vague results. You can't fix ambiguity downstream. So you learn to specify upfront — what does good look like, what are the edge cases, what are we not building. Not because you're a process person. Because you got burned enough times.

That instinct transferred almost directly. Before touching code, I'd work through the data model, the edge cases, the interfaces — talking it through with an AI assistant, poking holes before they became problems. That's exactly what I used to ask senior engineers to do before starting a project. I'd just stopped thinking of it as a management habit.

Scoping worked the same way. Before building anything, I'd ask: what's the simplest version that proves the approach works? Not the full feature — the smallest slice that would tell me if I was on the right track. I learned that in planning meetings, not at a keyboard.

**What management actually trains you for is working with systems that don't always do what you expect.** The analogy isn't perfect — you can ask a person why they got something wrong, and they can explain. Models can't. But the habits it builds turn out to be the right ones.

The instinct I had to relearn was going deep on implementation. Years of delegating had made me reach for help too fast — to an engineer before, to an AI assistant now. I caught myself doing it. Managing for a decade teaches you to watch yourself. That helped.

I didn't expect the management years to be useful here. I thought they were a detour. They weren't.
