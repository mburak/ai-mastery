I started this in late 2025 with a specific expectation: go deep on AI, get faster. Less guessing, fewer dead ends, more confident decisions. That part worked. I'm noticeably faster than I was.

What I didn't expect is everything else.

<!--more-->

The biggest shift isn't speed — it's knowing what I don't know. Before, I had blind spots I couldn't see. I'd make an architecture decision, it would work, and I'd move on without knowing whether I'd gotten lucky or whether I'd understood something. Now the blind spots have edges. I can point to them. I know I don't fully understand how models behave at context limits, how to design for reliability at scale, what the internals are actually doing when the output degrades. Knowing what you don't know is how you start making better decisions instead of better guesses.

The way I read about AI has changed too. Technical blog posts about model architecture used to lose me halfway through. Now I can follow the argument, even when I miss some details. More practically: when I'm evaluating architecture options for something I'm building, I can think through tradeoffs instead of defaulting to whatever pattern I've seen before.

The confidence shift is quieter but real. When someone recommends a model or an approach, I can ask what they're optimizing for — latency, cost, quality — and understand the answer. When someone describes a compound AI system I can ask about the failure modes between components. I'm not nodding along as much. I'm contributing to the conversation instead of mostly listening.

**What hasn't changed: I still treat the models as black boxes in practice.** I understand more about what's happening inside them than I did. But when I'm building, I'm still working mostly from behavior — what they do, not why. The gap between conceptual understanding and working intuition is bigger than I thought it would be at this point.

Two months in, I know enough to know how much I don't know yet. That's not a complaint. That's the point.
